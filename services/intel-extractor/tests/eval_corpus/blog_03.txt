Watering Holes Are Back, and They Brought a Budget

By the Argus Point Intelligence Team

We spent most of last year telling customers that strategic web compromise was a dying art.
We were wrong, and this post is the correction.

In March we responded to an intrusion at a European engineering firm that began, of all
places, at an industry association website the victim's employees visit daily. The
association's site had been modified to profile visitors and selectively redirect a small
subset of them, based on IP range and browser fingerprint, to an exploitation server at
203.0.113[.]61. Everyone else got the normal site. That selectivity is why the compromise
survived for nine weeks before anyone noticed.

The redirect chain ended at a domain the actor clearly registered for this campaign,
quiet-harbor[dot]example, which served a browser exploit to a small number of profiled
targets and a blank page to everyone else. The exploit itself was not novel; the targeting
discipline was. Fewer than forty machines worldwide ever saw the second stage.

We defang indicators in prose as a matter of house style; refang them before adding them to
your blocklists. And if you run a niche industry association website, understand that your
security posture is now part of your members' attack surface. That is the uncomfortable
economics of watering holes in 2026: the attacker compromises one under-resourced site and
inherits the trust of every well-resourced organization that visits it.
