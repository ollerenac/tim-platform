Why Your CDN Allowlist Is Lying to You

By Cordelia Venn, Principal Researcher

Here is a pattern we have now seen in four unrelated intrusions this year, which makes it a
trend and not an anecdote: attackers are naming their infrastructure to exploit the implicit
trust that security teams place in content delivery networks.

The most recent case is illustrative. An intrusion at a media company exfiltrated roughly
sixty gigabytes of pre-release content over two weeks, in small encrypted chunks, to a host
whose name was chosen with obvious care: bracken-cdn.example. Every analyst who triaged the
alerts saw the letters c-d-n and moved on. The traffic pattern, steady small uploads to a
"CDN," read as telemetry or cache synchronization to three separate reviewers before someone
finally asked why a CDN was receiving uploads at all.

That question is the entire lesson of this post. Content delivery networks deliver content.
They are download infrastructure. Sustained uploads to anything named like a CDN should be
treated as exfiltration until proven otherwise, no matter how boring the hostname looks.

We increasingly believe that naming psychology is an underrated attacker skill. Blocklists
and reputation feeds will always lag a freshly registered name, but an analyst's attention
is the scarcer resource, and a well-chosen hostname buys the attacker exactly the analyst
inattention they need. Audit your egress by behavior, not by vibes.
