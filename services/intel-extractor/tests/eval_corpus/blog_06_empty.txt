The Negotiation Table Has Moved: Ransomware Economics in 2026

By the Argus Point Intelligence Team

For years, the standard advice on ransomware negotiation was simple: stall for time, verify
the attacker can actually decrypt, and never let the first offer anchor the discussion. That
advice assumed a marketplace with some minimal honor among thieves. This year broke the
assumption.

Three separate developments changed the economics. First, the collapse of two major
affiliate programs flooded the market with experienced operators who no longer share revenue
upward, which means smaller demands but far more incidents. Second, insurers tightened
coverage language to the point where many victims can no longer count on reimbursement,
which attackers have noticed and priced in. Third, and most consequentially, several
jurisdictions moved from discouraging ransom payments to outright prohibiting them for
critical infrastructure operators, taking the largest and most reliable payers off the
table entirely.

The result is a strange new equilibrium. Median demands are down. Median dwell time is also
down, because operators who cannot count on a payout monetize whatever they can reach in the
first days rather than staging a careful, weeks-long extortion. Data theft without any
encryption at all now accounts for a majority of the incidents we work, because it is
cheaper to execute and the legal exposure it creates for the victim does the negotiating on
the attacker's behalf.

What should defenders take from this? The window between initial access and impact is
shrinking, so detection speed matters more than it did even a year ago. And the
conversation with your board should no longer be about whether to pay, but about how the
business absorbs the disclosure of whatever an intruder can reach in seventy-two hours.
Plan for that, and the negotiation table takes care of itself.
